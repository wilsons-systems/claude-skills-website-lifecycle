# Rora — Creative Direction

**Document status:** Production reference  
**Last updated:** May 2026  
**Owner:** Ryan Wilson, Pier 7 Projects Ltd

## The Four-Axis Framework

Every creative decision Rora makes — a headline, a colour choice, a layout, a line of copy — sits at the intersection of four axes. These axes don't describe aspirations. They describe constraints. When something feels wrong, come back here first.

---

### Axis 1 — Tone Register: Conversational

**Position:** Warm, first-person, contractions acceptable — but never playful, never flippant, and never the cold professionalism of enterprise software.

**What this means in practice:**

Rora speaks the way Ryan speaks — directly, without performance. Not "we are delighted to offer" and not "hey, let's crush it together." Somewhere in between: honest, specific, unhurried. A business owner talking to another business owner over coffee, not across a boardroom table.

Contractions are fine ("it's", "you're", "we've") because removing them creates distance. Short sentences are preferred because they signal confidence. The text doesn't need to fill space — it needs to say the right thing and stop.

First-person plural ("we") is used sparingly and only when genuinely accurate. "We built this for ourselves" is true and earns trust. "We're passionate about your success" is neither.

**Why this position, not another:**

A colder register — the kind you see on enterprise SaaS sites — signals that Rora is one of hundreds of tech vendors. That's a positioning failure. An overly casual register undermines the premium craft positioning and alienates the ICP (tradespeople and small business owners who are sceptical of "techies").

The conversational-but-grounded register is the one that allows Ryan's actual story to land: someone who ran a business, felt the pain, built the fix. That story only works if it sounds like it comes from a person.

**Reference brands:**

- **Basecamp (Hey.com)** — direct, plain, opinionated. Copy that sounds like someone wrote it, not a committee. Steal: the willingness to say what something is NOT as confidently as what it is.
- **Notion (early, pre-enterprise)** — warm without being saccharine. Clear benefit statements before feature lists. Steal: the "you" orientation, every sentence aimed at one reader.
- **Owl Labs** — talks to real people about real problems without condescension. Steal: the specificity ("save 3 hours a week" beats "increase efficiency").

### Axis 2 — Aesthetic Philosophy: Editorial Restrained

**Position:** Generous whitespace, single definitive type treatment, low colour count — signals confidence and patience, not Polished Standard SaaS.

**What this means in practice:**

The page breathes. Layout choices are deliberate, not space-filling. Type does the heavy lifting — one typeface, one weight in most contexts, type scale that guides the eye without forcing it. Colour appears rarely and earns its place. Nothing is decorated for decoration's sake.

This is not minimalism as a style. It is minimalism as a consequence of editorial discipline. A magazine like Kinfolk or a brand like Aesop doesn't look spare because the designers ran out of ideas — it looks spare because every element present had to justify its existence.

For Rora, this philosophy translates to: one column of copy before you scroll, a headline and a single supporting sentence. No cards stacked four across. No icon rows. No feature grids with 12 bullets. Each section of the site has one job, and the design enforces that.

**Why this position, not another:**

"Polished Standard SaaS" — the style of gradient-heavy hero sections, floating UI screenshots, feature-comparison tables, and stock photography of diverse people in open-plan offices — is the aesthetic that Rora's ICP has learned to distrust. It signals: expensive, complicated, probably won't work for a business like mine.

The editorial restrained aesthetic signals the opposite. It says: we thought carefully about this. We're not trying to impress you with visual noise. The quiet confidence of the design mirrors the quiet confidence of the product offer.

**Why navy/cream/coral wins over periwinkle:**

Periwinkle (#6C8EEF) is a capable colour but it carries precise cultural baggage: it is the signature tone of a generation of SaaS tools — Notion, Linear, Figma, dozens of product-led growth companies. For a designer audience, it reads as thoughtful. For a plumber in Blackpool, it reads as "another tech thing."

Navy (#1B2430) reads as authority, craft, and permanence. It's the colour of good stationery, a well-made jacket, a reliable van. Cream (#F5F2ED) is not white — it's warm and editorial, closer to paper than a screen. The combination immediately signals "different from tech" without announcing it.

Coral (#E8A38A) is the decision that makes the palette distinctive. It is warm, human, and specific — not the salmon of a wellness brand, not the orange of a startup, but a dusty, considered tone that functions as an accent without demanding attention. It appears on one element at a time: a button, a mark, an underline. Never as background, never as a text colour.

The periwinkle → navy/cream/coral shift is not a cosmetic upgrade. It is a repositioning move.

**Reference brands:**

- **Aesop** — type-first layouts, no photography on landing pages, cream and dark as the default. The product images don't compete with the copy. Steal: the confidence to leave sections empty except for one well-chosen sentence.
- **Mubi** — editorial grid, film stills used as art direction not decoration, typographic hierarchy that earns attention at every level. Steal: the use of caption-sized text to carry weight that most brands would put in a headline.
- **Stripe (pre-2022)** — before the gradient-heavy rebrand, Stripe was a masterclass in editorial restraint for a technical product. Steal: the willingness to explain how something works rather than just claiming it works.

### Axis 3 — Audience Relationship: Peer

**Position:** Ryan talks to business owners AS a business owner who solved the problem. Not expert-to-client. Not vendor-to-buyer. Peer-to-peer.

**What this means in practice:**

The copy never talks down to its reader and never positions Rora as the expert dispensing wisdom to the uninitiated. It positions Ryan as someone who was exactly where the reader is, who got frustrated enough to build a solution, and who is now sharing it.

This changes nearly everything: the pronoun orientation, the level of assumed knowledge, the structure of the pitch. Instead of "we provide AI automation for small businesses," it's "I used to spend 47 minutes on every quote. Now it takes four." Instead of "our platform is designed to streamline your operations," it's "the admin doesn't go away — it just stops being your problem."

The peer relationship also defines what Rora promises and what it doesn't. A peer doesn't promise the world. A peer says: here's what I did, here's what it changed, here's whether it might work for you. The assessment process (the call, the discovery) is framed as a genuine conversation, not a sales funnel.

**Why this position, not another:**

The alternative positions are: expert authority (Rora knows best, trust the system) or friendly helper (we're here to support your journey). Both are wrong for the ICP.

Tradespeople and local business owners are independent. They are not looking for an authority to defer to — they are looking for someone who understands their specific situation and has earned the right to make a recommendation. The peer relationship is the only one that earns that trust.

It also protects against the inevitable comparison to off-the-shelf tools. A vendor-to-buyer relationship invites "why shouldn't I just use Zapier?" A peer relationship invites "tell me more about what you built."

**Reference brands:**

- **Paul Jarvis (Company of One)** — personal brand built entirely on the peer relationship. Every piece of content is "here's what I learned." Steal: the habit of leading with personal experience before making any recommendation.
- **Gumroad (Sahil Lavingia era)** — CEO talks openly about building the business in public, including failures. Peer relationship with the creator community. Steal: the transparency that builds trust without undermining authority.
- **Webflow University** — educational content structured as "here's what I made" not "here's what you should do." Steal: the show-don't-tell orientation — demonstrations over claims.

---

### Axis 4 — Sensory Ambition: Quiet Precision

**Position:** Nothing shouts. Whitespace earns trust. Details signal confidence. Not quiet = forgettable, but quiet = deliberate.

**What this means in practice:**

Every micro-decision — paragraph spacing, line height, the weight of a border, the timing of a hover state — is made with care and restraint. The effect is cumulative: a visitor cannot point to one thing and say "that's what made me trust them," but the overall experience creates a feeling of craft.

Quiet precision means:
- Hover states exist and are considered, but they don't animate dramatically
- Borders are 1px and the right shade of navy-faded, not 2px grey
- Spacing is based on a 4-point or 8-point grid, never eyeballed
- Images, if present, are chosen for what they signal, not for coverage
- Motion, if present, is purposeful: a tooltip revealing context, a form field confirming success — not a hero section rotating through three taglines

The site should feel like it was made by someone who cares enough to get the small things right. That care, for an SMB audience, is proxy evidence that the product itself is built the same way.

**Why this position, not another:**

High visual ambition (dramatic motion, bold colour clashes, oversized typography used for effect) works for brands that need to interrupt attention. Rora's ICP is not scrolling Instagram — they're doing research. They came to the site looking for a reason to trust something. Drama gives them a reason to leave.

Low visual ambition (plain, functional, no considered aesthetic) is equally wrong. It signals that Rora didn't think about how it presents itself — which, for a brand selling professional systems, is a credibility failure.

Quiet precision threads the needle: enough craft to earn trust, enough restraint to keep the focus on the content.

**Reference brands:**

- **Linear** — micro-interactions that reward attention, keyboard navigation that actually works, type scale that never shouts. Steal: the 1px detail work, the way focus states are handled with care rather than as an afterthought.
- **Vercel** — dark mode by default, code that looks like it matters, zero superfluous decoration. Steal: the confidence to let negative space do work rather than filling every section.
- **Mast Brothers (chocolate packaging)** — analogue example. The craft is in the material, the texture, the restraint. Every detail is considered. There are no stickers or bursts. Steal: the idea that the absence of noise IS the signal.

## Synthesis: The Direction in One Paragraph

Rora looks and sounds like a business owner who got good at something, wants to share it, and didn't hire a marketing agency to tell you about it. The design is quiet enough to let the content be heard. The copy is direct enough to be believed. The palette is warm enough to feel human. The typography is precise enough to feel professional. Everything is chosen, nothing is defaulted. A visitor to the site should feel — without being able to explain why — that whoever built this paid attention. That feeling is the product.

## Rejection List

These are categorical decisions. Discussing them case-by-case is not productive — they are banned.

**Visual:**
- Gradients on any element (buttons, backgrounds, text, icons)
- Hero section with video background
- Floating UI screenshots on gradients (the "SaaS product screenshot" aesthetic)
- Countdown timers or urgency widgets of any kind
- Stock photography of laptops, people in offices, handshakes, "diverse team" imagery
- Animated number counters ("10,000+ businesses trust us")
- Excessive animation — page transitions, particle effects, cursor trackers
- More than two font weights visible on one page
- Blue-purple colour palette (periwinkle, lavender, violet — all excluded)
- Dark-to-light gradient CTAs
- "Card" grids with more than three items per row

**Copy:**
- "Seamless", "robust", "cutting-edge", "next-level", "revolutionise", "game-changer"
- Passive voice in headlines or CTAs
- More than one exclamation mark anywhere on the site (ideally zero)
- "AI-powered" as a modifier (say what the thing does)
- "And more..." as a list terminator
- Rhetorical questions used to open sections ("Tired of doing everything yourself?")
- Social proof numbers without a source or context ("97% of customers see results")
- Any claim that cannot be verified by Ryan's own experience

**Structural:**
- Pop-up modals within 30 seconds of landing
- Chat widgets that open automatically
- Cookie consent banners that obscure content
- "Book a call" as the first CTA before any context is established
- Navigation menus with more than 5 items
- Footer with 4+ columns and 20+ links
